Card Mechanic
===============================================================================
An important feature of the game world
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Mag-Stripe Cards

Plastic, card-stock (most common), or even heavy paper...
Most are a standard size, similar to a credit card in our world.
But the technology is more central to the game world, so their mag stripes are
more densely packed. They have more bits per card than we do.

They might also put multiple stripes on the same card. Side-A, Side-B style.
There are even 4-sided cards with two stripes on each side of the card.
Side A and Side B are accessed by flipping the card on the long axis. Side C
and Side D are accessed the same way after rotating the card 180 degrees in
the "yaw" direction.

Some cards are longer. I imagine something like old mag-stripe boarding passes.
Those often had a detachable stub that was used to staple the cards into a
jacket or folder. I imagine magazines coming in this format. A booklet of
extra-long cards with illustrations and photos from the articles printed on
their extra space.

These extra-long cards are no problem for swipe-style readers. Most insert-style
readers will not handle them correctly. Special-purpose insert-style readers
exist, but not generally for consumers.

# Readers

Everyone calls them "readers", but read-only devices are very, very rare.
Nearly every device reads and writes mag-stripe cards.

Reader/Writers come in two main styles: swipe and insert.

## Swipe-Style

Swipe-style readers are not as robust as insert-style readers, but they are
far more popular. Portable devices like MAGiE use them almost exclusively.

Because the swipe relies on the operator's movements to pull the data across
the read/write head, some skill is needed to get good results. Especially when
writing to cards.

## Insert-Style

Insert-style readers are more robust due to protections from dust, etc. But,
because they are usually installed in high-traffic areas, they actually break
down more often than the swipe-style readers. So people tend not to trust them.

The terms "terminal" and "kiosk" are generally used interchangeably. In truth,
a "kiosk" is mostly an installation style and location for a special-purpose
terminal. The Mallowance Kiosk is a great example.

## The Mallowance Kiosk

Near the mall entrance closest to the school is The Mallowance Kiosk. It
is nearly indistinguishable from an ATM in our world. Its functions are very,
very similar. The important distinction being in the dispensing.

The most-common happy-path use case for an ATM goes something like this.
The user inserts (or swipes) their card, enters a PIN, requests some cash,
and the machine dispenses it.

To collect their Mallowance, students insert their ID card. Then, the kiosk (or
"terminal") checks the ID and if all is in order it returns the ID before
dispensing a pre-determined number of credits on an additional card.

So the card slot serves a dual purpose. This is the case with most terminals,
but children rarely interact with terminals other than the Mallowance Kiosk.
